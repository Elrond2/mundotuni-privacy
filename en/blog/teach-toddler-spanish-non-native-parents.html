How to Teach Your Toddler Spanish If You Don’t Speak It

You can give your toddler regular Spanish exposure without being fluent yourself, though no source we found promises fluency. What the research points to is how much Spanish your child hears and whether it comes through real back-and-forth.

Yes, you can bring regular Spanish into your toddler’s life without being fluent yourself, but the research we reviewed offers no guarantee of fluency. Two things stand out: how much Spanish your child hears compared with English, and whether it arrives through live, back-and-forth exchanges with real people. Both can be worked on from wherever you are starting.

The short version

  • In studies of bilingual toddlers, progress in a language tracked how much of it the child heard.
  • Toddlers in lab studies learned from people who responded to them, not from recordings or one-way video.
  • Short daily routines, native-speaker voices and a live Spanish speaker now and then are realistic goals.
  • An app can add Spanish sound to the week, but it doesn’t replace you or another person.

Can you raise a Spanish-speaking child without speaking Spanish?

It depends on what you mean by “raise.” If the goal is a toddler who hears Spanish most days, recognizes it and enjoys it, that is within reach for many families. If the goal is full fluency, none of the sources below says a parent who doesn’t speak the language can get a child there alone, so we won’t promise it.

The studies we cite also have limits you should know about. They are small, several were done in a lab, and none tested a family like yours, with a single English-speaking household adding Spanish. Read them as clues about what matters, not as a recipe.

What does research say about exposure?

Place and Hoff (2011) studied 29 bilingual toddlers at 25 months. The relative amount of exposure to each language predicted how well the child did in that language. For English, the number of different speakers and the share of input from native speakers also predicted proficiency. Hoff and colleagues (2012) followed bilingual children aged 22 to 30 months and found that every measure of vocabulary and grammar in a language was related to how much input the child received in it.

In plain terms: in these studies, more Spanish in the child’s week went with more Spanish from the child. This is an association, not proof that adding hours causes the gain. The abstracts we read give no target number of hours, so we won’t invent one.

The finding about native speakers is worth a second look if you are not one. The study doesn’t say what it means for a parent learning the language, so what follows is our inference: it is a reason to add native voices to your child’s week instead of relying only on your own Spanish.

Why does live interaction matter?

Kuhl and colleagues (2003) tested 9-month-old American babies on speech sounds from Mandarin. The babies learned them from live speakers but not from audio or video recordings. Roseberry and colleagues (2014) tested 36 children aged 24 to 30 months on new verbs. The children learned them in interactions that responded to them, whether in person or by video chat, and not from video that didn’t respond.

The limits are real. The samples are small, the settings are labs, and the babies in the first study were far younger than a child using Spanish at home. Our reading is modest: a person who answers your child counts for more than a recording that plays on its own, and a video call with a Spanish-speaking relative can be responsive. These studies don’t show that recordings or apps are useless.

What everyday routines help?

The ideas below are ours. None of the studies tested them. They follow from the two findings above: more Spanish, and more of it with a person who responds.

  • Pick one anchor moment. Snack time, the bath or a bedtime book, always with a little Spanish, is easier to keep up than a vague plan to “use more Spanish.”
  • Use words you are sure of. Short sentences about what you are both looking at (“the dog,” “more,” “open”) beat long ones you have to look up.
  • Borrow native voices. Play songs and audio books in Spanish, then sing along, point and pause so your child answers you. That turns a recording into something you do together.
  • Find a live speaker. A Spanish-speaking relative, friend, babysitter, playgroup or class, or a scheduled video call, adds the human conversation the studies point to.
  • Keep it playful. Skip quizzes. If your child answers in English, accept it and carry on in Spanish.

The Communication Health Support Association (CHSA), endorsed by ASHA, advises speaking to your child in the language or languages you feel most comfortable with. That fits this approach: keep English for everyday talk if that is your natural voice, and add Spanish in the moments and through the people that make it manageable.

Where does an app fit, and where doesn’t it?

An app can put Spanish sounds and words in front of your child and give you vocabulary to reuse. It is not a live speaker, and none of the studies above tested apps, so we can’t tell you how much one adds.

A review of educational apps (Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues, 2015) names four qualities to look for: active, engaged, meaningful and socially interactive. The last one is the quality an app can’t supply by itself, and you can. Sit next to your child, repeat the words, ask “where is it?” and let the game start a conversation.

Which Spanish should you choose?

Spanish sounds different in Spain and in Latin America, and it is fine to wonder which one to use at home. We cover it in Spain or Latin American Spanish: which for your child? If Spanish is your family’s heritage language rather than a new one, raising bilingual kids and keeping Spanish alive is the better starting point.

Frequently asked questions

Should I speak only Spanish at home if I’m not fluent?

You don’t have to. CHSA advises speaking to your child in the language or languages you are most comfortable with. A realistic plan is English for everyday talk, with Spanish at set times, in books and songs, and through other speakers. That plan is our suggestion, not a research finding.

Will learning Spanish delay my child’s English?

CHSA says learning more than one language doesn’t cause delay or confusion. A child’s Spanish and English may each develop a little differently depending on how much of each they hear. For more detail, see our article on whether bilingualism causes speech delay.

Is it okay if my child mixes English and Spanish?

Yes. CHSA describes mixing languages as a normal part of learning more than one. It isn’t a sign that your child is confused or that the second language is failing. If your child answers in English, you can keep speaking Spanish and not turn it into a correction.

Do Spanish songs and videos count as exposure?

They add Spanish sound, but the studies we cite found toddlers learned from interactions that responded to them, not from one-way video. Use songs and videos as a shared activity: sing, point, repeat words and ask questions. Add live conversation when you can, even by video call.

What if my child seems slow to talk?

According to CHSA, a communication problem shows up in every language a child uses, and learning two languages is not the cause. If you are worried, talk to your pediatrician or a speech-language pathologist. Don’t wait to see whether the extra language explains it.
