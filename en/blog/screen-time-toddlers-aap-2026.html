Screen Time for Toddlers: What the AAP and the WHO Say Now

No single number is shared by every major group. The WHO says no more than one hour a day at ages 2 to 4. The AAP’s 2026 policy sets no universal limit and looks at content, use and app design as well.

There is no single number that every major group agrees on. The World Health Organization (WHO) says children ages 2 to 4 should have no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day, and that less is better. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) replaced its older guidance in January 2026 with a policy that sets no universal time limit, although it still offers less than an hour a day as a range for toddlers and preschoolers. Both treat time as something that matters, and neither says screens are harmless.

The short version

  • WHO (2019): no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day for ages 2 to 4. Less is better.
  • AAP (2026): no universal limit. For families who want a number, limits might range from under 1 hour a day for toddlers and preschoolers.
  • The two are different bodies answering different questions, so they do not line up exactly. We do not pick between them.
  • Most of the evidence is observational. It shows links, not proof of cause.

What changed in the AAP’s guidance in January 2026?

On January 20, 2026, the AAP announced a policy statement and a technical report titled “Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents” (Pediatrics, February 2026). According to the AAP, they replace its earlier guidance on communications and media. The abstract says the subject “cannot be viewed solely” through individual children’s behavior or “screen limits alone.” The reason it gives is that digital platforms built around engagement and commercial goals often encourage long use, and long use can displace healthy habits such as movement and sleep.

Reading “not solely” as “not at all” would be a mistake. The same statement says that longitudinal and meta-analytic studies show “consistent links between more time spent with digital media and less optimal child development.” It also says that the research is largely observational and that the associations are correlational, so they do not establish cause. For families who want a number, the AAP says time limits might range from under 1 hour a day for toddlers and preschoolers.

The old rule, and why you still see it

The AAP’s 2016 guidance recommended avoiding digital media before 18 months, with video chatting as the exception, and limiting it to 1 hour a day from ages 2 to 5. In the search results we reviewed, many parenting pages still repeat that rule as current.

A transcript of a February 2026 Reach Out and Read podcast adds a detail. The interviewer remarks that “two years” and “two hours a day” are no longer in the policy, and lead author Tiffany Munzer answers “Yes, exactly.” That is an interview, a secondary source. The policy text still mentions a range under one hour.

The WHO says one hour. Does that contradict the AAP?

The WHO’s April 2019 guidelines on physical activity, sedentary behavior and sleep for children under 5 say that, at ages 2 to 4, sedentary screen time should be no more than one hour and that less is better. They also encourage reading and storytelling with an adult. The AAP policy answers a different question: how children grow up in a digital environment shaped by companies. The two come from different organizations, years and scopes. The WHO sets a ceiling. The AAP declines a universal ceiling while naming under an hour as a possible range.

That’s the guidance. If your family does use screens now and then, the same guidelines explain how to make that time better.

The AAP’s 5 C’s for toddlers and preschoolers

The AAP’s questions for families of toddlers and preschoolers are organized around five C’s: Child, Content, Calm, Crowding out and Communication. Four of them line up with statements in the AAP’s 2026 texts and its family pages.

  • Content. In ages 0 to 5, the policy links high-quality educational content with more prosocial behavior and stronger language, and says the effect grows when a caregiver watches or plays along. It links heavy non-educational use and solo use with delays in language, thinking, social-emotional skills, executive function and fine motor skills, and with poorer sleep. These are associations from mostly observational studies.
  • Calm. The AAP’s toddler page advises trying not to distract a child with a device whenever they are upset or bored. A 2023 study of 422 children ages 3 to 5 found that using devices to calm them went with more emotional reactivity six months later in boys and in children with more intense temperaments. The authors suggest it may displace chances to learn how to manage feelings. It is one six-month study with parent-reported data, so it shows an association, not a cause.
  • Crowding out. The AAP says prolonged use can displace movement and sleep.
  • Communication. The AAP’s technical report says that even high-quality programming and co-viewing do not give children the same benefit as device-free interaction with attentive caregivers. The policy adds that children will not miss out on learning if families delay giving them a tablet, and the toddler page advises against open access to a tablet or phone.

Ads, autoplay and design: what to look for

The AAP names specific features: autoplay, algorithmic recommendations and intermittent rewards. It notes that gaming apps often include easy advancement and frequent badges or trophies, and it asks that children’s apps use less frequent reinforcement and include chances to check in so a child can stop. It describes low-quality content as content with “dark design patterns,” embedded ads or excessive interactive elements.

One US study describes how common one of them is. A 2019 content analysis found that 129 of 135 popular Google Play apps for children under 5 had at least one kind of advertising, as often among apps labeled educational as among the rest. The data date from 2018 and 2019, and app stores change. The study counts ads, not harm to children.

Regulators are looking at the same features. In July 2025 the European Commission published guidelines asking online platforms to disable by default features that contribute to excessive use, such as streaks, autoplay and push notifications. The guidelines are not binding and address online platforms, so they are context, not a rule for any one app.

The AAP is explicit that this is not a parenting failure. It says the digital ecosystem is shaped by industry incentives and lies largely outside the control of individual families, and it tells pediatricians to avoid fueling caregiver guilt. Before you download an app or hand over a device, you can check:

  • Are there ads, including ones disguised as part of the game?
  • Can your child reach in-app purchases?
  • Does the next video or level start on its own?
  • Do frequent badges or trophies pull your child to keep going?
  • Is there a natural stopping point, or a moment that invites your child to stop?

A calm plan for your family

The AAP recommends a family media plan, including screen-free times and places such as the car and the dinner table. If a number helps you set boundaries, the WHO’s one hour and the AAP’s under-an-hour range are the two reference points to start from, and your pediatrician can help you adjust them for your child.

Time without screens can be language time too: reading and storytelling with an adult is what the WHO encourages. In a bilingual home, our guide to keeping Spanish alive at home has ideas for that time. If you are concerned about your child’s speech, sleep or behavior, talk with your pediatrician or ask about early intervention services in your state. See also whether two languages cause a speech delay.

And in Mundo Tuni?

We don’t claim screens are harmless. The WHO advises no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day for ages 2 to 4. Spain’s pediatric association goes further and recommends avoiding screens from birth to age 6. We report both as they are.

If your family does use an app, the AAP suggests high-quality content, using it together, and not relying on it to calm your child. It also points out that many apps are designed to keep kids playing, largely outside a family’s control.

Frequently asked questions

How much screen time is OK for a 3-year-old?

The WHO advises no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day at ages 3 to 4, and says less is better. The AAP’s 2026 policy sets no universal limit. For families who want a number, it says limits might range from under one hour a day for toddlers and preschoolers. Your pediatrician can help you apply either one to your child.

Did the AAP drop its one-hour rule?

Not entirely. The 2026 policy replaced the earlier guidance and sets no universal limit, but it still offers under an hour a day as a possible range for toddlers and preschoolers. It also asks families to move beyond simple limits and focus on quality, context and conversation. It does not say that more screen time is fine.

Do educational apps help toddlers learn?

The AAP links high-quality educational content with stronger language and more prosocial behavior, especially when a caregiver joins in. It also says that even high-quality programming and co-viewing do not match device-free time with attentive caregivers. These findings are associations from mostly observational studies. They do not promise results for any single child.

Is it OK to give my child a device to calm a tantrum?

The AAP’s toddler page advises trying not to distract children with a device whenever they are upset or bored. A 2023 study of 3- to 5-year-olds linked calming with devices to more emotional reactivity six months later in some children. It shows an association, not a cause, and it is a guide, not a verdict on any family.
