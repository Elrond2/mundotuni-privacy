Spain or Latin American Spanish: Which for Your Child?

Neither is the “right” Spanish. They are varieties of one language, and the best choice is usually the one your child will hear most from the people around them.

Neither one is the right Spanish. Spanish from Spain and from Latin America are varieties of the same language, with differences that are well known but that nobody should rank. For most families the practical answer is to choose the variety your child will actually hear, regularly, from the people in their life.

The short version

  • Spain and Latin America share one language; the known differences include pronunciation and some words and forms of address.
  • We found no basis for calling either variety easier or better, and we don’t.
  • Family, community and travel plans are better guides than the label.
  • In studies of bilingual toddlers, progress in a language tracked how much of it the child heard, so steady exposure is worth more thought than the label.

Are they different languages?

No. Both are Spanish, and speakers of different varieties generally understand each other. This is widely known linguistic background, and we cite no study for it. The differences below are the kind you hear and see when comparing varieties, and we describe them without putting numbers or judgments on them.

What actually changes?

The best-known differences are in how words sound, in some everyday vocabulary and in grammar. A familiar example is the plural “you.” In most of Spain, people use vosotros with friends and family and ustedes in more formal settings. In most of Latin America, ustedes covers both. Pronunciation also varies from region to region, and Latin America is not one accent either.

We don’t cite a linguistic reference for these points. They are standard descriptions of Spanish, but we haven’t checked them against an academic source for this article. Treat them as general background, not as research findings.

For a child aged 2 to 6, the practical question isn’t which grammar is correct. It is which words and sounds your child will hear every day.

What about Spanish in the United States?

If you live in the US, Spanish is part of daily life for many families. The Census Bureau reported that in 2019 Spanish was the most widely spoken non-English language in US homes (62%), and that 67.8 million people spoke a language other than English at home. Pew Research Center found that 68% of US Latinos spoke Spanish at home in 2024, down from 78% in 2000.

Neither source says which variety people speak, so they can’t tell you which one to choose. They do show that Spanish is widely present in US homes.

How do you decide: family, community or travel?

These are our suggestions, not research findings. Start with the person who will speak to your child most. If a grandparent, a nanny or a neighbor speaks a particular variety, that is probably your answer, because your child will hear it for real. Then look at your community: the teacher, the playgroup or the library story hour may favor one variety. Travel plans can break a tie. If you visit a specific country every year, that country’s Spanish will be useful.

If none of this points anywhere, pick the variety of the audio and books you can get easily and stick with it for a while. The aim is to keep the exposure steady. Hoff and colleagues (2012) found that, in bilingual toddlers aged 22 to 30 months, every measure of vocabulary and grammar in a language was related to how much input the child got in it. That study measured exposure to each language, not to a variety of Spanish, so it can’t tell you which to choose.

If Spanish is already spoken in your family, the choice is mostly made for you: your child’s home variety is the one to keep. Our article on raising bilingual kids and keeping Spanish alive looks at how families hold on to it from one generation to the next. If your child understands Spanish but answers in English, this guide may help more than any question about accents. Either way, who talks with your child every day is a better guide than the name of the variety.

One caution applies to all of this. A variety is not a fixed menu you must pick from in advance. Children hear what the people around them say, and families change over time: a move, a new teacher or a trip can shift which Spanish your child hears most. Treat the choice as a starting point you can revisit, not a permanent commitment.

Is mixing accents a problem?

The sources we used don’t address children who hear two varieties of one language, so we can’t say it helps or hurts. What we do have is guidance on two languages. The Communication Health Support Association (CHSA), endorsed by ASHA, says learning more than one language doesn’t cause delay or confusion and that mixing languages is normal. Our inference, flagged as such, is that hearing a grandmother from one country and a teacher from another is unlikely to be a problem in itself. If you have a concern about your child’s speech, ask a pediatrician or a speech-language pathologist.

Frequently asked questions

Is Spanish from Spain or Latin American Spanish easier for kids?

The sources we reviewed don’t compare the two, so we can’t say either is easier, and we don’t think a ranking would help you. What the research on bilingual toddlers does connect to progress is how much of a language a child hears, so the variety you can offer consistently is a sensible choice.

Will hearing two kinds of Spanish confuse my child?

We found no evidence on this. For two languages, CHSA says learning more than one doesn’t cause confusion and that mixing is normal. We haven’t seen that guidance applied to two varieties of one language, so treat any answer, including ours, as an educated guess. A concern about speech should go to a professional.

Which should I pick if I have no family or community ties to either?

Pick the one whose voices, songs and books you can get easily, and keep using it for a while. Steady exposure is the point. Think about where you might travel or who your child will talk to in a few years, but don’t let the decision stall you. You can add the other variety later.
