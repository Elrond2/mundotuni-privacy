Raising Bilingual Kids in the US: Keeping Spanish Alive at Home

Spanish tends to fade across generations in US families, and what research links to kids speaking it is mostly how much they hear it at home. There is no single method, but there are well-supported places to start.

Keeping Spanish alive in a country where English dominates school and daily life takes steady, practical effort, not a special method. The data show why it matters: Spanish use drops from one immigrant generation to the next. Research suggests that the lever best supported by evidence is exposure, meaning how much Spanish your child hears and from how many people, and that rules like “one parent, one language” are not required.

In short

  • Pew surveys show Spanish use at home declining across generations, though they don't explain why.
  • In several studies, the relative amount of input in a language goes with a child's skill in it.
  • “One parent, one language” was neither necessary nor sufficient in one large study.
  • Speak the language you feel most comfortable in. Learning two languages does not cause a delay.

Why does Spanish fade across generations?

A Pew Research Center survey from 2015 asked Latino parents about the language they use with their children. Among immigrant parents, 97% said they speak Spanish to their children. In the second generation the figure was 71%, and among the third generation and later it was 49% (Pew, 2018).

The trend is also visible in the wider population. Pew reports that the share of US Latinos who speak Spanish at home fell from 78% in 2000 to 68% in 2024 (Piña and Martinez, 2025).

These are survey results about what people say they do. They don't tell us why the numbers fall, and they say nothing about any one family. Our own reading is that, as the studies below suggest, a child who hears less Spanish at home tends to end up with less Spanish, so a thinner share of Spanish in daily life across a generation can add up. That is an interpretation, not something Pew tested.

What predicts whether kids speak Spanish?

Three studies point the same way: how much a child hears a language goes with how well they do in it, and with whether they use it.

  • In 29 bilingual toddlers at 25 months, relative exposure to each language predicted proficiency in that language. For English, the number of different speakers and the proportion of native-speaker input also mattered (Place and Hoff, 2011).
  • In bilingual children aged 22 to 30 months, all the vocabulary and grammar measures in a language were related to the relative amount of input in it (Hoff and colleagues, 2012).
  • In a study of 1,899 families, parents' input patterns at home correlated with whether the children used the minority language. When both parents spoke it, the likelihood was high (De Houwer, 2007).

Keep the limits in view. The first two studies are small and look at toddlers. De Houwer studied families in Flanders, Belgium, not in the US. All three show associations, not proof that more input causes the outcome.

Which home strategies hold up, and which one is a myth?

The same Belgian study offers the most useful correction for families who have heard that “one parent, one language” is the only way. In its data, that pattern was neither necessary nor sufficient for children to use the minority language. What did show up was both parents speaking it.

A second worry is that raising a child in two languages might slow their speech or confuse them. CHSA, endorsed by ASHA, says that learning more than one language does not cause delay or confusion and that mixing languages is normal. We cover this in more detail in Does being bilingual cause speech delay?

Speak the language you're comfortable with

CHSA's advice is to speak to your child in the language or languages you feel most comfortable with. For some families that is Spanish all day. For others it is a mix, or English with some Spanish, and the studies above suggest it is worth being deliberate about how much Spanish your child hears. If one parent doesn't speak Spanish, we have a separate piece on teaching a toddler Spanish when you don't speak it.

Books, songs, family and community

No study we reviewed ranks these, so what follows is our suggestion rather than a finding. If exposure is the lever, then anything that adds real Spanish to your child's week is worth considering: bedtime books in Spanish, songs in the car, regular calls with grandparents and other relatives, and playdates or activities with other Spanish-speaking families. The research on exposure we cite linked hearing more different speakers with English proficiency; it did not compare that with spending more time with one speaker.

If your child understands but doesn't answer in Spanish, we look at that situation in My child understands Spanish but answers in English.

Where do apps fit?

An app can add some Spanish to a week, and that is its limit. It can't stand in for conversation with the people around your child, and we found no study showing that an app keeps a language alive. Think of it as a supplement to what happens at home.

Frequently asked questions

Does my child need both parents to speak Spanish?

In De Houwer's study of 1,899 families in Belgium, children were highly likely to use the minority language when both parents spoke it to them. The “one parent, one language” rule was neither necessary nor sufficient. It's a correlational, non-US study, so treat it as a useful clue.

Will speaking Spanish at home delay my child's English?

CHSA, endorsed by ASHA, says that learning more than one language doesn't cause delay or confusion. The studies we reviewed also link a language's skill level to how much of it a child hears, which is why English exposure matters for English. If you have concerns, consult a speech-language pathologist.

Is it a problem if my child mixes Spanish and English?

According to CHSA, mixing languages is normal when children learn more than one. A communication problem would show up in all the languages the child uses, not only in the mixing. If you're unsure about your child's development, ask a speech-language pathologist or your pediatrician.

What if Spanish isn't my strongest language?

CHSA advises speaking to your child in the language or languages you feel most comfortable in. Spanish can still reach your child through other relatives, books and songs. In the research we cite, the number of different speakers was linked with English proficiency; the study did not compare that with more time with one speaker.

Why does Spanish decline in later generations?

Pew's surveys show that fewer parents speak Spanish to their children in later generations: 97% of immigrant Latino parents, 71% in the second generation and 49% in the third or later. The surveys don't explain why. Studies of toddlers link skill in a language to how much of it they hear.
