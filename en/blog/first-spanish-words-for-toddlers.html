First Spanish Words for Toddlers: Where to Start

Start with a small set of words tied to things your child sees and does every day, and use them again and again in real moments. Frequency and context matter more than a long list.

Begin with a small set of Spanish words that match your toddler’s day: greetings, family, food, animals and the little words of routines. Say them often, in the moment they apply, rather than in a long list. In one study of bilingual toddlers, vocabulary and grammar in each language went with how much of that language the child heard, so repeated, real use is what to aim for.

The short version

  • Pick five to ten words that belong to your daily routines and use them every day.
  • Say the word while the thing is happening or in your hand, not as a quiz.
  • Spain and Latin America share most of these words; a few differ, and either choice is fine.
  • Mixing Spanish and English is normal for bilingual children and doesn’t cause delay or confusion, according to CHSA and ASHA.

Why start with a small set of words?

Hoff and colleagues (2012) followed bilingual children between about 22 and 30 months old. In their results, every measure of vocabulary and grammar in a language was related to the relative amount of input the child had in that language. This is an association, not proof that more input causes more words, and the study doesn’t tell us how many words to teach.

Our reading is practical: if what counts is how much Spanish a child hears, ten words used all day will probably give more than a hundred words seen once. That is our suggestion, not a finding from the study, and it makes no promise about how fast any child will learn.

If you don’t speak Spanish yourself, a short list is also easier to keep up. You will find more ideas in our guide on teaching your toddler Spanish if you don’t speak it.

Which Spanish words for toddlers come first?

There is no official first-words list, and the lists below are ours. We chose common words that are correct in Spain and in Latin America, and we flag the ones that vary. Start with the theme where your child already shows interest.

Greetings and manners

Hola (hello), adiós (goodbye), gracias (thank you), por favor (please), buenos días (good morning) and buenas noches (good night). They fit into moments that repeat every day.

Family

Mamá, papá, abuela and abuelo (grandma and grandpa), hermano and hermana (brother and sister) and bebé. Affectionate forms such as abuelita or mami are widely used too, and which one a family prefers varies.

Food and drink

Agua (water), leche (milk), pan (bread), manzana (apple), queso (cheese) and más (more). Here the two regions diverge a little, as the table shows.

Animals

Perro (dog), gato (cat), pájaro (bird), pez (fish), vaca (cow) and caballo (horse). Animals pair well with books, toys and walks.

Body and routines

Manos (hands), pies (feet), nariz (nose), boca (mouth), ojos (eyes), baño (bath or bathroom) and pañal (diaper). Because they come up during dressing, bathing and changing, they get natural repetition.

Two accents, same words

Most of the words above are identical in Spain and Latin America. A few everyday words differ, and some differ from country to country within Latin America, so treat this as a sample rather than a rule.

bananaplátanobanana or plátano, depending on the country
carcochecarro or auto, depending on the country
“all done”ya estálisto

Pronunciation differs as well. In most of Spain, c before e or i and the letter z sound like the “th” in “thin,” so gracias and cinco have that sound. In most of Latin America, the same letters sound like an English “s.” This is widely known background rather than something we tested.

Neither version is better, and your child can hear both. If you want help choosing, we walk through it in Spain or Latin American Spanish: which for your child. If you are unsure how a word sounds, listen to it in an online dictionary with audio before you use it.

Use Spanish words in daily routines, not as flashcards

Words stick to moments. Say agua as you hand over the cup, más when your child signals for another bite, and adiós at the door. A flashcard asks a toddler to produce a word out of context, which isn’t how most toddlers learn.

A few habits help:

  • Say the Spanish word once, naturally, and keep going. Don’t ask your child to repeat it.
  • Use the same word for the same thing for a while, so the pairing is easy to catch.
  • If your child answers in English or mixes the two, that is fine. Keep using the Spanish word.

On mixing, CHSA, which is endorsed by ASHA, says that learning more than one language doesn’t cause speech or language problems and that mixing languages is a normal part of bilingual development.

Play ideas for each theme

These are our own suggestions, not tested programs.

  • Greetings: let a stuffed animal say hola and adiós when it arrives and leaves. Your toddler can wave back.
  • Family: look through a photo album and say mamá, papá or abuela while pointing.
  • Food: at snack time, name each item as it goes on the plate, then offer más or ya está or listo, depending on which version you prefer.
  • Animals: make a pretend farm with toys and put each animal to bed with its name: buenas noches, vaca.
  • Body: sing or chant while touching each part, and swap in the Spanish name. For more language play at this age, see how to support your toddler’s language through play. Spanish songs and nursery rhymes work well here too: seven traditional ones to start with.

Frequently asked questions

How many Spanish words should a toddler learn?

None of the sources we use gives a target number, and we won’t invent one. In the study above, progress in each language followed how much of it the child heard. Rather than counting words, focus on hearing a few of them every day. Children differ, and your child doesn’t have to follow anyone’s list.

Does mixing Spanish and English confuse my toddler?

According to CHSA, endorsed by ASHA, learning more than one language doesn’t cause speech or language problems, and mixing languages is a normal part of bilingual development. A toddler who says a Spanish word inside an English sentence is using what they have. You can simply answer with the word in the language you chose.

Should I use flashcards for Spanish words?

You can, but we’d put them second. Toddlers tend to connect words with real objects and moments, so saying agua while pouring water offers more than a card does. If your child enjoys cards, use them as one more way to name things and keep the pressure off. Neither source we use studies flashcards, so this is our suggestion.

Which Spanish should I teach, from Spain or Latin America?

Either works, and most basic words are the same. Choose the variety your child will hear most from family, community or travel, and stay with one word per object at first, such as jugo or zumo. Hearing both later is not a problem. Our article on Spain and Latin American Spanish goes deeper.

My child isn’t talking much. Could Spanish be the reason?

According to CHSA, a communication problem shows up in all the languages a child uses, and learning two languages doesn’t cause one. If you’re worried about your child’s speech or language, talk to your pediatrician or a speech-language pathologist. You can also ask about the early intervention services in your state.
