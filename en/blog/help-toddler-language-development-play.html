How to Help Your Toddler’s Language Development Through Play

You help language along by having real back-and-forth conversations while your child plays: follow what interests them, answer, name what they do and pause so they can reply.

To help your toddler’s language development through play, you don’t need flashcards or special toys. You need a real conversation: notice what your child is interested in, respond, put words to what they are doing, and leave a pause so they can answer. The research we looked at points to the back-and-forth more than to the number of words, though the studies are small.

The short version

  • In a small study of 4- to 6-year-olds, conversational turns tracked language-related brain activity more closely than word counts.
  • Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child calls these exchanges “serve and return” and describes five steps that fit into any kind of play.
  • Open-ended questions work best mixed with comments, with a few seconds of quiet for your child to reply.
  • Every child has their own pace. If something worries you, ask your pediatrician.

Is it the number of words or the conversation that matters?

For years the advice was to talk to your child as much as possible. A 2018 study by Romeo and colleagues added nuance. They recorded the home audio of 36 children aged 4 to 6 and later scanned their brains with functional MRI while the children listened to a story. Children who had taken more conversational turns with adults showed more activity in Broca’s area, a region tied to language, and that activity partly explained the link between their language experience and their verbal skills. The result held after accounting for socioeconomic status, IQ and how much the adults talked.

It is an observational study of 36 children, so it shows an association, not that conversation changes the brain. The title itself questions the “30-million-word gap” figure, so we don’t use that number here.

The same group ran a 2021 randomized trial with 52 children aged 4 to 6: a nine-week family program versus a control group. Conversational turns rose more in the program group, and that rise was linked to gains in language and executive function. It is also small, and the program was not “playing at home” as such, so it does not prove the ideas here. Neither tested a particular game, and both involved children older than toddlers.

What is “serve and return,” and how do you do it?

The Center on the Developing Child at Harvard uses “serve and return” for responsive, back-and-forth exchanges between a young child and a caring adult, and says they play a key role in shaping brain architecture. As in tennis, your child “serves” with a look, a babble or a word, and you return the ball. A reproduction of the Center’s guide lists five steps:

  1. Notice the serve and share your child’s focus of attention. If they point at the truck, look at the truck. Don’t change the subject.
  2. Return the serve by supporting and encouraging. A smile, an “Oh, that’s big!” or repeating what they said already counts as a reply.
  3. Give it a name. “That’s a fire truck, and it has a ladder.”
  4. Take turns, and wait. Say something, then stay quiet for a few seconds. That gap is where your child talks.
  5. Practice endings and beginnings. Close each exchange clearly, with a goodbye for example, then open the next one.

We read a reproduction of the Center’s guide, not the original, so take the steps as a summary.

What can you say at each age?

The CDC’s “Learn the Signs. Act Early.” pages list language milestones and tips for each age. The milestones were revised with the American Academy of Pediatrics in 2022 and reflect what at least 75 percent of children do by a given age (Zubler and colleagues, 2022). They are reference points, not a test.

Around 30 months: describe, name and play with opposites

At 30 months the CDC lists saying about 50 words and combining two or more words with a verb, like “doggie run.” Its tips include describing what you see with opposites (big and little, fast and slow, in and out) and asking simple questions about stories.

At 3 years: who, what, where and why

At 3, the CDC milestones include talking with you in conversation using at least two back-and-forth exchanges, and asking “who,” “what,” “where” or “why” questions. Faced with a stream of “why?”, answer briefly, then toss it back: “What do you think?”

At 4 and 5 years: “What happens next?”

At 4, the CDC lists sentences of four or more words and talking about at least one thing that happened during the day. It suggests asking what might happen next in a story or game, and making time for their “why” questions. At 5, the milestones are keeping a conversation going for more than three exchanges and telling a story, heard or made up, with at least two events. The tip is to ask about their play and follow up with “why” and “how.”

What open-ended questions can you ask while they play?

An open-ended question can’t be answered with “yes,” “no” or a single word. The examples below are our own ideas, inspired by the CDC’s tips. They are not a validated protocol, so swap in the words you use at home.

  • Play kitchen: “What are you cooking?” “What should we do with the soup next?” “How do you know it’s ready?”
  • Blocks: “How will you keep it from falling?” “Tell me about what you built.” “What would happen if we added one more on top?”
  • Toy cars: “Where are they going?” “What happens if they meet in the tunnel?” “Why did it stop?”

What gets in the way of the conversation?

Three habits tend to slow a conversation down. The first is the quiz, one question after another (“What color is this? And this? And this?”). Mix questions with comments (“That’s blue, like your shirt”) and let your child lead. The second is correcting every word. If they say “the dog runned,” answer naturally, “Yes, the dog ran really fast,” without asking for a repeat. The third is not leaving time. If you answer before they finish thinking, there is no turn left for them to take.

These three tips are common sense, not findings from the sources cited here.

Does it matter that the conversation is live?

Roseberry, Hirsh-Pasek and Golinkoff (2014) worked with 36 toddlers aged 24 to 30 months. The children learned new verbs only through contingent interaction, meaning someone responding to them in the moment, either in person or over video chat. They did not learn them from video that ignored them. It is a small study of verb learning and did not test apps or games, so it says nothing about them. It fits the idea that responding matters.

What if you speak Spanish, or two languages, at home?

Everything above works in any language. The Communication Health Support Association (CHSA), with the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA), advises speaking to your child in the language or languages your family is most comfortable with, and says a communication problem shows up in all the languages a child uses. We cover this in does bilingualism cause speech delay.

When should you talk to a professional?

The CDC milestones are a reference, not a diagnosis. If you are worried about how your child talks, or you feel they communicate less than you expected, talk to your pediatrician, who can look into it and, if needed, point you to early intervention services in your state. Frequently asked questions

What is “serve and return”?

It is Harvard’s Center on the Developing Child’s name for responsive, back-and-forth exchanges between a young child and a caring adult. According to the Center, these exchanges help shape brain architecture. In practice, you notice, respond, name it and wait.

How many words should a 2½-year-old say?

The CDC lists saying about 50 words at 30 months, and combining two or more words with a verb, like “doggie run.” It is a general reference, not a cutoff. Questions about how your child talks are for your pediatrician.

At what age can a child hold a conversation?

By the CDC’s milestones, a 3-year-old talks with you using at least two back-and-forth exchanges, and a 5-year-old keeps a conversation going for more than three. These are US reference points, and each child follows their own timeline, so keep talking together.

Is it enough to talk to my child a lot?

Not on its own. In a study of 36 children aged 4 to 6, conversational turns with adults were linked to language-related brain activity more closely than the number of words heard. The study is small and shows an association, not a cause, but it suggests that the back-and-forth itself counts for something.

What should I ask my child while they play?

The CDC suggests asking what might happen next in a game or story (age 4), and asking about their play and following up with “why” and “how” (age 5). Mix questions with comments about what they are doing, and leave a few seconds of quiet so they can answer.

Does it help to talk while we use an app together?

Possibly, though none of the studies we reviewed tested it. A review of educational apps proposes “socially interactive” learning as one of four pillars (Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues, 2015). That is a proposed framework, not a trial. Roseberry’s toddler study did not test apps.
