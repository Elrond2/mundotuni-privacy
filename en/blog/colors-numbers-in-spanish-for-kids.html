Colors and Numbers in Spanish: Play Ideas for Toddlers

Teach colors and numbers in Spanish by using them in play you already do: hunting for red things, counting snacks, climbing stairs. A few words used often beat a long list.

The easiest way to teach colors and numbers in Spanish is to use them inside games and routines you already have: spotting rojo (red) at the park, counting uno, dos, tres as you climb the stairs. Start with a few colors and a few numbers, repeat them in real moments, and don’t quiz. The guide below gives the words with both accents and some play ideas.

The short version

  • Begin with the colors and numbers your child already meets every day, a handful at a time.
  • Spain and Latin America share most of these words; a few colors and the pronunciation of some numbers differ.
  • CDC milestones are a guide, not a test: they describe what most children do by a given age.
  • Games, not flashcards: touch, sort, hide, count and name the thing in front of you.

Colors in Spanish, with both accents

Most color words are the same in Spain and Latin America. A few have more than one common form, and which one you hear depends on the country, so use this table as a guide and not as a rule. Our word choices are ours, not an official list.

redrojoSame everywhere.
blueazulIn most of Spain the z sounds like the “th” in “thin”; in most of Latin America it sounds like “s.”
yellowamarilloSame everywhere.
greenverdeSame everywhere.
orangenaranjaSome places also say anaranjado.
pinkrosaSome places say rosado.
purplemoradoVioleta and púrpura are also used in some places.

One detail you don’t need to teach but may notice: color words that end in -o change with the noun. It is el coche rojo (the red car) but la pelota roja (the red ball). Azul, verde and naranja don’t change for masculine and feminine nouns. Just say the phrase naturally and your child will hear the pattern.

When you count things, uno becomes un or una to match the noun: un perro (one dog), una vaca (one cow). The difference between “th” and “s” is widely known background, and you can use either one. If you’re deciding, see our guide on Spain and Latin American Spanish.

What can you expect at each age?

The CDC’s milestones (Learn the Signs. Act Early.) offer a few reference points. At 4 years, the list includes “Names a few colors of items.” At 5 years it includes “Counts to 10” and “Names some numbers between 1 and 5 when you point to them.”

The milestones, revised with the AAP in 2022, record what 75% or more of children do by each age (Zubler et al., 2022). So some children will get there later. Milestones are not a diagnosis: if your child isn’t meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills, or you have concerns, talk with their pediatrician and ask about developmental screening. The CDC lists don’t tell us how milestones apply to a second language, so we shouldn’t treat them as Spanish targets. They are a general picture of development.

What can be said about a second language comes from Hoff and colleagues (2012). In bilingual children between about 22 and 30 months, vocabulary and grammar in each language were related to how much of that language the child heard. That is an association, but it points to the same everyday advice: if you want your child to have colors and numbers in Spanish, they need to hear them.

Games for colors and numbers

These are our own suggestions. None has been tested as a program, and none is meant to teach in a fixed number of days.

Color games

  • Veo, veo (I spy): “Veo, veo, algo de color verde” (I spy something green). Let your child point, and say the color again when they find it. Take turns so they can set the puzzle too.
  • Color hunt: call out amarillo and look around the room for yellow things. Put them in a basket.
  • Laundry or toy sorting: sort socks, blocks or crayons into piles and name each pile: rojo here, azul there.
  • Snack colors: name the color of fruit or vegetables as you cut them: rojo for the strawberry, verde for the pear.

Counting games

  • Counting snacks: set out crackers or grapes and count them as you touch each one: uno, dos, tres. Then eat them and count the empty plate: cero (zero).
  • Stairs and steps: count the stairs on the way up, and on the way down.
  • Hide and seek: the seeker covers their eyes and counts to three, or ten, in Spanish. Then call ¡Ya voy! (here I come).
  • Toy lineup: line up cars or stuffed animals and count them together. Then switch the order and count again.

Saying it naturally

Name things in short phrases: la pelota roja, tres galletas. Skip the quiz questions like “What color is this?” if they cause frustration. Say the answer yourself, in the moment, and move on. If your child answers in English, accept that and repeat the Spanish word once. CHSA, which is endorsed by ASHA, says that mixing languages is a normal part of bilingual development and doesn’t cause problems.

Frequently asked questions

Which colors should I teach first in Spanish?

No source we use says which colors come first, so choose the ones your child sees all day: the red cup, the blue shoes, the yellow banana. Two or three are plenty to start. Add more when your child points to or names these on their own. There is no set order, and there is no deadline.

How high should we count in Spanish?

The CDC lists “Counts to 10” among the milestones at age 5, so 10 is a common reference point, though nothing requires it. Many families start with uno, dos, tres and add numbers over time as counting stays fun. Count real things you can touch, so each number goes with one object.

My child knows colors in English. Does Spanish add anything?

It adds exposure to Spanish, and that is what the research we found points to: in bilingual toddlers, skills in each language went with how much of that language the child heard (Hoff et al., 2012). Knowing colors in English doesn’t stop your child from learning the Spanish words. Just name the same colors in Spanish during play.

Do I need to use the same accent every time?

No. Children can hear more than one way of saying a word. If you want to keep things simple at first, choose one form, such as marrón or café for brown, and use it for a while. Mixing later is not a problem. We discuss the choice in our article on Spain and Latin American Spanish.

When should I ask a professional about my child’s language?

Ask if you’re concerned. According to CHSA, endorsed by ASHA, a communication difficulty shows up in all the languages a child uses, and learning two languages doesn’t cause it. Your pediatrician can guide you, and a speech-language pathologist or your state’s early intervention services can evaluate your child.
