Spanish for Toddlers and Preschoolers: Where to Start (Ages 2 to 6)

A toddler can start hearing Spanish at any age, whether or not you speak it. What the research we reviewed links to progress is how much Spanish a child hears and whether it comes from people who respond. This guide gathers the basics and points to our detailed articles on each step.

Adding Spanish to a toddler’s life doesn’t need a fluent parent, a special method or a particular age. The pediatric and speech-language sources we read say young children can learn more than one language and that doing so doesn’t cause delay. The studies we cite point to two things you can work on: how much Spanish your child hears, and whether it comes through real back-and-forth with people. None of them promises fluency, and we won’t either.

The short version

  • Any age works. Very young children pick up languages easily, and older children and adults can learn them too, with frequent exposure and practice (AAP and ASHA).
  • In studies of bilingual toddlers, progress in a language tracked how much of it the child heard.
  • Toddlers in lab studies learned from people who responded to them, not from one-way recordings.
  • Start small: a handful of everyday words, a few songs, one daily Spanish moment and a live Spanish speaker when you can.
  • An app can add Spanish to the week. It doesn’t replace you or another person.

Can a toddler learn Spanish at home if you don’t speak it?

A toddler can hear Spanish most days, recognize it and enjoy it even if you are not a speaker. Full fluency is another matter: none of the sources below says a parent who doesn’t speak Spanish can get a child there alone. What you can do is bring in Spanish through routines, native voices in songs and books, and people who speak it.

The Communication Health Support Association (CHSA), endorsed by ASHA, advises talking to your child in the language or languages you are most comfortable with. For many families that means English for everyday talk and Spanish in set moments. We go step by step in how to teach your toddler Spanish if you don’t speak it.

What does research say about how toddlers learn a second language?

Two findings come up again and again in the studies we reviewed.

  • Amount of exposure. Place and Hoff (2011) studied 29 bilingual toddlers at 25 months: the relative amount of exposure to each language predicted how well the child did in it. Hoff and colleagues (2012) followed bilingual children from 22 to 30 months and found every measure of vocabulary and grammar in a language related to how much input the child received in it. These are associations, not proof that adding hours causes the gain, and the studies give no target number of hours.
  • Live, responsive interaction. Kuhl and colleagues (2003) found that 9-month-old American babies learned Mandarin speech sounds from live speakers but not from audio or video recordings. Roseberry and colleagues (2014) found that children aged 24 to 30 months learned new verbs from interactions that responded to them, in person or by video chat, and not from video that didn’t respond.

The samples are small and several studies took place in a lab, so read them as clues about what matters, not as a recipe. Our reading: more Spanish in the week, and more of it with someone who answers your child.

When should you start teaching your toddler Spanish?

Whenever you can. The AAP and ASHA page for parents explains that young children appear to learn languages easily and quickly because of rapid brain development between birth and age 3, but that older children and adults can also learn additional languages. Their bottom line is that languages can be learned at any age, with frequent exposure and regular practice. CHSA, writing for families who already use more than one language, advises using them with your child often and as early as possible.

So a 2-year-old is not too young, and a 5-year-old is not too late. Once you begin, aim for frequent exposure and regular practice.

Which Spanish words and phrases come first?

Start with a small set of words tied to what your child sees and does every day: food, family, animals, body parts, “more,” “all done,” “hello.” Say the word while the thing is happening or in your hand, and repeat it in real moments rather than as a quiz. We suggest themes and play ideas in first Spanish words for toddlers, and games with colors and numbers in Spanish.

Songs, books and daily routines

CHSA suggests reading picture books together, singing and talking about the lyrics, and watching children’s shows in different languages. In the specific tasks tested by Kuhl and Roseberry, children learned from live or responsive interactions but not from the recordings used. These studies don’t show that recordings are useless, but they are a reason to join in: sing along, point, pause so your child can answer, and turn a recording into something you do together. Seven traditional songs to start with are in Spanish songs for toddlers.

The routines below are our suggestions, not tested methods:

  • One anchor moment a day. Snack time, the bath or a bedtime book, always with a little Spanish.
  • A live speaker now and then. A relative, babysitter, playgroup, class or a scheduled video call.
  • No pressure. If your child answers in English, accept it and carry on in Spanish.

Spain Spanish or Latin American Spanish?

Neither is the “right” Spanish. They are varieties of one language, and the usual tiebreaker is which one your child will hear most from family, community or travel. We found no basis for calling either easier or better. The differences and how to decide are in Spain or Latin American Spanish: which for your child?

If your child understands Spanish but answers in English

On its own, this is not a sign of a language problem. A real difficulty would show in every language the child uses (CHSA; AAP and ASHA). What research links to a child speaking a language is how much of it they hear and from whom; where English fills school and the street, Spanish can end up with the smaller share of the week (our reading, not something the studies measured). We collect gentle ideas in my child understands Spanish but answers in English. If Spanish is your family’s heritage language, raising bilingual kids and keeping Spanish alive goes further.

Where do apps fit, and what should you check?

An app can put Spanish sounds and words in front of your child and give you vocabulary to reuse. It is not a live speaker, and none of the language studies above tested apps. A review of educational apps (Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues, 2015) suggests looking for apps that make a child active, engaged and mentally involved, that connect to their life and that invite social interaction. The last part is yours to supply: sit with your child, repeat the words and let the game start a conversation.

Design matters too: ads, purchases a child can reach, autoplay and endless rewards work against calm play. Our eight questions for choosing an educational app go through them, and our screen-time article covers how much screen time pediatric guidance suggests.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 2-year-old too young for Spanish?

No. The AAP and ASHA page for parents says young children appear to learn languages easily and quickly, and that languages can be learned at any age with frequent exposure and regular practice. At 2, keep it to words, songs and play.

Do I need to speak Spanish myself?

No, though you will need other sources of Spanish. CHSA advises speaking to your child in the language you are most comfortable with. Add Spanish through songs, books, native speakers and set moments of the day. That plan is our suggestion, not a research finding.

Will learning Spanish delay my child’s English?

CHSA and the AAP and ASHA page say learning more than one language doesn’t cause speech or language delays. Each language may grow a little more slowly on its own, while the total number of words across both is about the same as a child learning one language. More detail in does bilingualism cause speech delay?

Which Spanish should I teach: Spain or Latin America?

The one your child will hear most from the people around them. Both are the same language. The sources we read don’t study children who hear two varieties, but our inference is that hearing both is unlikely to be a problem in itself. See Spain or Latin American Spanish.

How much Spanish does my toddler need each day?

We found no research-based number of minutes or hours. The studies we cite link progress to the share of Spanish a child hears and to live, responsive interaction, not to a daily target. Steady exposure most days, with people who answer your child, is the realistic goal.
