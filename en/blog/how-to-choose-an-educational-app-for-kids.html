How to Choose an Educational App for Kids Ages 2 to 6: 8 Questions

The label “educational” does not, by itself, show that an app has been tested for learning benefits. These eight questions, drawn from child-development research and the AAP’s 2026 policy, help you spot the design that teaches and the design that hooks.

A good educational app for a toddler or preschooler asks your child to think, keeps them on one task, connects to something they already know, and leaves room for you to join in. The label alone tells you little: in 2015, a team of child-development researchers described apps sold as educational as largely unregulated and untested. So it helps to look at the design yourself, in about ten minutes, before your child ever touches the app.

In short

  • Researchers judge an app by whether it makes a child active, engaged, and mentally involved, whether it connects to their life, and whether it invites social interaction.
  • The AAP’s 2026 policy puts design that prolongs use (autoplay, frequent rewards, ads) at the center of the problem.
  • This checklist is about design. How long a child plays still matters, and no app replaces time with an adult.

What makes an app educational, according to research?

In the 2015 review “Putting Education in ‘Educational’ Apps,” Kathy Hirsh-Pasek and colleagues applied what is known about how children learn to apps. They proposed four pillars. A good app keeps a child active, thinking rather than just tapping. It keeps them engaged, without distractions that pull them away from the task. It presents something meaningful, connected to what they know and to their own life. And it supports social interaction, for example by giving you something to talk about afterward. All of this should serve a clear learning goal.

This is a framework built from a review of the science of learning, not a trial of particular apps on children. Its use is that it turns a vague promise into things you can check.

What does the AAP’s 2026 policy say about app design?

On January 20, 2026, the American Academy of Pediatrics published a policy statement and a technical report titled “Digital Ecosystems, Children, and Adolescents.” The policy says that digital environments built around engagement and commercial goals often encourage prolonged use, and that long use can displace healthy habits such as movement and sleep. It names autoplay, algorithmic recommendations, and intermittent rewards among the features that do this, and it describes low-quality content as content with “dark design patterns” such as frequent rewards for play, embedded ads, or excessive interactive elements. It asks the industry to turn off autoplay and not to use targeted advertising on minors.

Two cautions. The policy is expert consensus, and the AAP itself notes that the research is largely observational, so it shows links rather than proof of cause. It also does not rate or endorse any particular app. And the AAP is explicit that this is not a parenting failure: it says the digital ecosystem is shaped by industry incentives and lies largely outside the control of individual families.

1. Does it show ads?

An ad in the middle of a game breaks concentration and often leads to another app or a website. A 2019 content analysis of 135 popular Google Play apps for children under 5 found that 129 of them had at least one kind of advertising, and that apps labeled educational were no different from the rest. Those data are from 2018 and 2019 and app stores change, but the answer you want has not: none, including ads disguised as part of the game.

2. Can your child buy something or leave the app?

Our rule of thumb, not a finding from the research: purchases, links to websites, and settings should sit behind something a young child cannot do, such as a math problem or a hold-and-press. Try to reach the store from inside a game. If a buy button appears between levels, that is your answer.

3. Does it hook or teach?

The policy’s glossary defines “engagement-based design” as digital designs that compete for a user’s attention, interaction, and data. The policy notes that gaming apps often include easy advancement and frequent badges or trophies to encourage play, and recommends that children’s apps use less frequent reinforcement. Watch for streaks that break if you skip a day, prizes that arrive at random, and sparkles every few seconds. A good app can still reward a finished activity. What matters is whether the reward serves the task or the task serves the reward.

4. Does the next thing start on its own?

Autoplay is on the AAP’s list of features to switch off, and its family guidance for toddlers says the same. If a new video, level, or round begins before your child has decided to continue, the app is making the choice for them.

5. Is the screen calm?

The AAP counts “excessive interactive elements” as a sign of low-quality content. Look at what is on the screen at any moment. A calm app, in our judgment rather than the research’s, asks for one thing at a time, leaves a few seconds to think and to get it wrong, and keeps sounds gentle.

6. Are the instructions understandable without reading?

Few children between 2 and 6 read. Instructions should come by voice or in clear pictures. If your child asks for help every few seconds to understand what to do, the app was not made for their age.

7. What happens to their data?

Read the privacy policy, not just the store summary. Does the app ask for your child’s name, birthday, or an account? Does it use advertising or tracking tools? The AAP asks the industry for safety and privacy by default and no targeted advertising on minors. For this age, the less an app collects, the better.

8. Is there a natural place to stop?

The AAP recommends that children’s apps build in chances to check in so a child can step away from play. Look for a gentle ending, a play timer you can set, and no “one more round” loop. If taking the device away always ends in a meltdown, the app may be part of the reason. The AAP’s family guidance suggests helping kids recognize that digital media has lots of “hooks” that keep us online longer than we intended.

Does time still matter?

Yes. A checklist about design does not replace limits. The World Health Organization advises no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day at ages 2 to 4, and says less is better. The AAP’s 2026 policy sets no universal limit, but for families who want a number it says limits might range from under one hour a day for toddlers and preschoolers. The AAP’s technical report adds that even high-quality programming and co-viewing do not give children the same benefit as device-free interaction with attentive caregivers. Our guide to the AAP’s 2026 policy and the WHO’s limit sets the two side by side.

Playing together is the fourth pillar in practice. After a session, ask what they found or made, and let them show you. Time with you matters for a reason the AAP gives: its technical report says device-free interaction with attentive caregivers gives children a benefit that even high-quality programming does not match. Our ideas for supporting language through play need no device at all.

The ten-minute test. Before handing it over, play it yourself. If within ten minutes you have seen an ad, found a way to buy something without a gate, watched something start on its own, or felt a reward pulling you on without knowing why, you have your answer.

Frequently asked questions

Are educational apps good for toddlers?

The evidence is limited and mostly observational. The AAP links high-quality educational content in ages 0 to 5 with more prosocial behavior and stronger language, especially when a caregiver joins in. It also says even high-quality programming does not match device-free time with attentive caregivers. These are associations, and they promise nothing for any one child.

What should I look for in an app for a 2-year-old?

Check for no ads, no autoplay, no streaks or random prizes, a calm screen, voice instructions instead of text, and a gate in front of purchases and settings. Play it yourself first. For this age, also look for a natural ending, so your child can stop without a fight. None of this replaces time with you.

How common are ads in apps for young children?

Very common in one US sample. A 2019 analysis found at least one type of advertising in 129 of 135 popular Google Play apps for children under 5, as often among apps labeled educational as among the others. The data are from 2018 and 2019, and the study counted ads, not harm to children.

Does the AAP recommend specific apps?

No. The AAP’s 2026 policy describes what to look for and what to avoid, such as autoplay, frequent rewards, and embedded ads, but it does not evaluate or endorse any particular app. A claim that an app is AAP-approved is not something the policy supports.

What are “dark patterns” in children’s apps?

They are design choices that steer behavior in ways that benefit the company. The AAP’s glossary defines engagement-based design as designs that compete for a user’s attention, interaction, and data, and lists persuasive, profit-driven, and manipulative design as synonyms. In its policy, frequent rewards for play are an example of a dark design pattern.
