My Child Understands Spanish but Answers in English: What to Do

Understanding Spanish and replying in English is not, on its own, the kind of problem that speech-language professionals describe. What research does link to a child using a language is how much of it they hear, and from whom.

Your child may understand Spanish perfectly well and still answer in English, and by itself that pattern is not what speech-language professionals describe as a warning sign. CHSA, a resource endorsed by the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA), says that a communication problem shows up in all the languages a child uses, not in just one. What studies do link to a child using a language is how much of it they hear and who they hear it from.

In short

  • Preferring English is not, by itself, a sign of a language problem. A real difficulty would show in both languages.
  • In the studies below, more relative exposure to a language goes with better skill in it.
  • No study we reviewed shows that pushing a child to answer in Spanish works, or that it backfires. We offer ideas, not guarantees.
  • If you are unsure about your child's communication in either language, ask a speech-language pathologist.

Why does my child understand Spanish but answer in English?

We did not find a study that counts how often this happens or pins down why, so we can't give you a percentage or a single cause. What the research does show is a steady link between exposure and skill. In a study of 29 bilingual toddlers at 25 months, the relative amount of exposure to each language predicted how well the child did in that language. For English, the number of different people the child heard and the share of input from native speakers also mattered (Place and Hoff, 2011).

A second study followed the same logic with a wider lens: in bilingual children between 22 and 30 months, every vocabulary and grammar measure in one language was related to the relative amount of input in that language (Hoff and colleagues, 2012).

Where English is the language of school and the street, Spanish can easily end up with the smaller share of a child's week. That is our reading of these studies, not something they measured, but it fits a child who follows Spanish and replies in English.

Is it a problem if they only answer in English?

According to CHSA, learning more than one language does not cause a delay or confusion, and mixing languages is normal. Its description of a communication problem is specific: it appears in every language the child uses. So a child who understands Spanish, expresses themselves well in English and simply answers in English does not match that description. We are reading CHSA here and can't assess your child; only a professional can do that.

The reverse case deserves attention. If your child struggles to understand or to express themselves in both languages, that is the situation CHSA flags, and the language they answer in is beside the point.

What does research link to speaking, not just understanding?

The clearest evidence we found on home language use comes from a large study with 1,899 families (De Houwer, 2007). The way parents used their languages at home correlated with whether the children used the minority language themselves. When both parents spoke the minority language to the child, the chances that the child used it were high. The popular “one parent, one language” rule turned out to be neither necessary nor sufficient.

Two limits matter. The families lived in Flanders, Belgium, not in the United States, and the study is correlational: it shows that input patterns and children's language use go together, not that one causes the other. Still, it points in the same direction as the exposure studies above. What counts is how Spanish is actually used at home, more than any rule about who speaks what.

Gentle ways to give Spanish more room

The ideas below are ours. They follow from the studies above but have not been tested as a way to get a child to start answering in Spanish.

  • Speak the language you feel most comfortable in. CHSA recommends this for any family raising a child with more than one language.
  • Attach Spanish to routines you already have, such as breakfast, bath time or the drive to school, so the exposure doesn't depend on finding extra time.
  • Add voices. The English results in Place and Hoff included the number of different speakers, so grandparents, cousins and family friends, in person or on a call, are a way to add voices. The study did not compare that with more time with one speaker.
  • Answer what your child means, then say it once in Spanish. If they say “I want milk,” you reply “Ah, quieres leche” and pour it. No do-over needed.

What about insisting that they answer in Spanish? The only claims we found about that come from parent forums, not from studies, so we make none. Choose what fits your child and your family, and watch how they react.

When to talk to a speech-language pathologist

Ask for an evaluation if you notice that your child has trouble communicating in English as well as in Spanish, if you have doubts about their development in either language, or if you simply want a professional opinion. CHSA's advice is to consult a speech-language pathologist when in doubt, and your child's pediatrician can point you to one. Bilingualism is not a reason to wait. We do not diagnose or give clinical advice here, and nothing in this article replaces an evaluation. If you are weighing the worry that two languages cause a delay, we looked at that question in Does being bilingual cause speech delay?

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for a child to understand Spanish but answer in English?

The studies we reviewed don't say how common it is, so we can't give you a figure. CHSA says that learning more than one language doesn't cause a delay or confusion and that mixing languages is normal. A communication problem, it adds, would appear in all the languages the child uses.

Does it mean my child is confused or behind?

Not according to CHSA, which says that learning several languages doesn't cause confusion or a delay. Answering in English while understanding Spanish is a language preference, and a real difficulty would show in both languages. If you have doubts about either one, a speech-language pathologist can evaluate your child.

Should I make my child answer in Spanish?

We found no solid research showing that insisting helps or backfires, so we can't promise either result. What studies link to a child using a language is how much they hear it and how it is used at home. A gentler place to start is more Spanish in daily routines and more Spanish-speaking people around.

Do both parents need to speak Spanish?

In De Houwer's study of 1,899 families in Belgium, children were very likely to use the minority language when both parents spoke it to them. The “one parent, one language” rule was neither necessary nor sufficient. The study is correlational and not from the US, so treat it as a clue, not a rule.

What if Spanish isn't my strongest language?

CHSA's advice is to speak to your child in the language or languages you feel most comfortable with. Relative exposure matters in the research, but nothing we reviewed says a parent should speak a language they don't feel at ease in. Other relatives, books and songs can add Spanish without putting the weight on you.

When should I see a speech-language pathologist?

When you have doubts about your child's communication, in either language. CHSA says that a communication problem appears in all the languages a child uses and recommends consulting a speech-language pathologist when in doubt. Your pediatrician can refer you, and you don't need to wait for the bilingualism question to resolve itself.
