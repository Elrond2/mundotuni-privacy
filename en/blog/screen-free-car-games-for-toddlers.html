Screen-Free Car Games for Toddlers and Preschoolers (and Waiting Rooms)

The games that work best in the car and in a waiting room use your voice and the view outside. Pack a small bag, plan a break about every two hours, and the AAP’s 2026 guidance even names the car as a screen-free place.

The easiest way to keep a toddler or preschooler happy in the car or a waiting room is to play with your voice and the view: songs, “I spy,” animal guesses, counting trucks. Add a small bag of toys and snacks, and in the car plan a break about every two hours. The AAP’s 2026 guidance asks families to choose screen-free times and places in advance, and it offers the car as an example.

In short

  • The AAP suggests planning a break about every two hours on a long drive.
  • If your child gets carsick, looking out the window helps, and books or games that fix their eyes inside the car do not.
  • The games below are popular ideas, not medical recommendations or research findings.
  • For children under 3 and for waiting rooms, keep it short, simple, and sung or spoken.

Why plan a screen-free car ride?

In January 2026, the American Academy of Pediatrics published new guidance on children and digital media. It recommends that each family make a media plan, including screen-free times and places, and gives the car and the dinner table as examples. The plan is the point: deciding beforehand is easier than negotiating from the back seat at mile 140. Whether to make an exception for a long trip is your call, and the games below work either way. Our guide to the AAP’s 2026 policy covers the wider picture, including the WHO’s one-hour limit for ages 2 to 4.

Before you leave: what to pack

The AAP suggests a bag of toys and snacks to keep your child busy on the trip (its flying advice says this, and the idea carries over to the car). Keep it to a few small things that are easy to handle with a seat belt on. One idea of ours, not the AAP’s: hand things out one at a time instead of all at once, so each new item lasts longer.

Car games by age

These are ideas from parents and teachers, not research. They need almost no equipment. Choose by what your child can already do, and keep it light: if a game stops being fun, switch.

Under 3: short, sung, and repeated

  • Songs with small gestures. Pick songs they know and use motions that fit in a car seat: clapping, waving, wiggling fingers.
  • Name what you see. “Big truck! Red car! Tree!” Say the word, then pause so they can point or repeat.
  • Animal sounds. “What does the cow say?” Swap turns so they ask you.
  • Peekaboo with a soft cloth, when another adult is in the back seat.

Ages 3 and 4: look, guess, and sing

  • “I spy” with colors. “I spy something blue.” The CDC lists naming a few colors of items as a milestone at age 4, so a younger child may just point. Milestones are a way to keep track of development, not a test for the car.
  • “Which animal am I?” Give one clue at a time: “I have four legs, and I say meow.”
  • Story swap. You start a sentence, your child finishes it, then you switch.

Ages 5 and 6: count and play with sounds

  • Count cars of one color. Pick a color and count how many pass before the next break. The CDC lists counting to 10 as a milestone at age 5. For more ways to practice counting, see our guide to teaching counting to toddlers and preschoolers.
  • First-sound “I spy.” “I spy something that starts with /m/.” Adapt it to what your child can already do.
  • Riddles and rhymes. Take turns, and let the rhymes be silly.

Breaks and safety

The AAP advises planning a break about every two hours so that you and your child can rest. That is a general guide: stop sooner if your child is restless or feels sick, as soon as it is safe to do so. The AAP also reminds families never to leave a child alone in a car, even for a minute. For car seat choice and use, follow the manufacturer’s instructions and your state’s law, and see the AAP’s travel page in the sources below.

If your child gets carsick

According to the AAP, pull over as soon as it is safe and let your child walk around a little. Then have them look at things outside the car and avoid books or games that make them focus on something inside. Radio, songs, and conversation can help. The AAP adds a light snack before the trip if it has been about three hours since your child ate. If carsickness is frequent or worries you, talk with your pediatrician; the only advice here about medicine is to ask them.

Our own inference from that advice: a screen inside the car asks your child to look inside, so it may work against it. The AAP’s page does not say this about screens in particular.

Waiting rooms, restaurants, and other short waits

A few things fit almost any room. Bring one or two small toys or a board book. Tell a short story or talk about what you can see. Whisper-talk games (“say it in your quietest voice”) fit a quiet room. For sedentary time, the WHO encourages reading and storytelling with an adult, which is easy to do anywhere you can sit. Our ideas for supporting language through play include phrases you can use while you wait.

On a plane

For ear discomfort on takeoff and landing, the AAP suggests breast, bottle, or pacifier for babies, and older children can try sipping through a straw. It also suggests a bag of toys and snacks. On tablets, the AAP writes that a tablet with videos can be a good backup if the other toys do not work, which makes it a backup and not plan A. On lap infants, many airlines allow a child under 2 on a lap, but when asked whether it is safest, the AAP answers that “the short answer is no.” It cites turbulence, which it names as the main cause of children’s injuries on planes, and recommends an approved child restraint.

Frequently asked questions

How often should we stop on a long drive with a toddler?

The AAP advises planning a break about every two hours so that both the driver and your child can rest. It is a general guide, not a rule. If your child is restless or starts to feel sick, stop sooner, as soon as it is safe to pull over.

What can I do if my toddler gets carsick?

Pull over when it is safe and let them walk a bit. Then ask them to look outside the car, and skip books or games that make them focus inside. Radio, songs, and conversation can help. If it happens often or worries you, ask your pediatrician about next steps.

What are good car games for a child under 3?

Short, simple, and sung or spoken: songs with small gestures, naming what you see out the window, animal sounds, and a soft toy to pass back and forth. These are popular ideas, not research findings. Keep each one brief, repeat what your child likes, and switch as soon as the interest fades.

How do I keep a toddler busy in a waiting room?

Bring a couple of small toys or a board book, tell a short story, and talk quietly about what you see. The WHO encourages reading and storytelling with an adult during sedentary time. Hand things over one at a time so each lasts longer. This last tip is ours, not from the research.

Can a child under 2 fly on an adult’s lap?

Many airlines allow it, but when asked if it is the safest option, the AAP says “the short answer is no.” It points to turbulence and recommends an approved child restraint system. Check your airline’s own policy for the details before you book.
