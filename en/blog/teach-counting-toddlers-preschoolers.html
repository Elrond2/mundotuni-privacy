How to Teach Counting to Toddlers and Preschoolers

Counting means saying the numbers in order and knowing that the last one tells you how many. Kids build that over the preschool years, and everyday routines give them plenty of practice.

The most reliable way to teach counting is to count real things together during the day: plates at dinner, stairs on the way up, grapes in a bowl. A child is counting for real when, besides saying the numbers in order, they understand that the last number they say is how many there are. Reciting “one, two, three” usually comes first and doesn’t guarantee the second. In the CDC’s milestones, “Counts to 10” appears at age 5, where it describes what 75 percent or more of kids can do.

The short version

  • Counting combines several ideas: the order of the numbers, one number per object, and “the last number tells how many.”
  • Very small groups can be recognized at a glance. Larger ones have to be counted.
  • Routines such as setting the table, shopping and climbing stairs offer plenty of chances to count with no prep.
  • Milestone ages are a guide to what is typical, not a deadline.

What does it mean to count?

Many toddlers recite the number sequence like a song well before they know what it is for. That is a normal first step, and the next one is giving the words meaning.

The four ideas behind counting

DREME, the early math network at Stanford University, breaks counting into four ideas:

  1. Order: the numbers always come in the same sequence.
  2. One to one: each object gets exactly one number, with none skipped and none repeated.
  3. The last number tells how many: the final number said is the total.
  4. Order of objects doesn’t change the amount: it doesn’t matter which toy car you start with, there are still the same number of cars.

A child can have the first idea and miss the second, pointing at three objects while saying five numbers. That is part of learning. It is worked out by counting real objects together, not by correcting.

Seeing “how many” without counting: subitizing

Subitizing is knowing how many objects there are at a glance, without counting them one by one. According to DREME, basic subitizing involves very small groups: one, two, three and maybe four. Larger quantities can sometimes be recognized by seeing smaller groups within them, such as two groups of three; otherwise, counting takes over. DREME treats it as an important and often neglected foundation of math. Practicing it takes no effort: “How many crackers are on the plate? Can you tell right away, or should we count?”

What can you expect at each age?

One caution before the ages. The CDC milestones describe what 75 percent or more of kids do by a given age (Zubler et al., 2022), so up to a quarter of kids haven’t yet, and that is a statistic, not a verdict. Milestones are not a diagnosis: if your child isn’t meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills, or you have concerns, talk with their pediatrician and ask about developmental screening. The only counting milestone we cite is at age 5. For younger ages, what exists is research and play ideas, not a table.

Toddlers: “one,” “more,” and counting together

For ages 2 and under, we have no milestone to cite and won’t make one up. What you can do is say the words in context (“one cracker, one more cracker”) and count out loud while you do something else. These are play ideas of ours, not targets.

Ages 3 and 4: small numbers come first

A lab study of 3- and 4-year-olds (Le Corre and Carey, 2007) found that “one,” “two,” “three” and “four” are tied to small sets of objects before kids master the principles of counting. Larger number words attached to approximate amounts later. It is one study, and it can’t be used to set exact ages. It may, though, explain why a child handles three crayons easily and gets lost with nine.

Around age 5: counting to 10 and naming numbers

The CDC lists two math milestones at age 5: “Counts to 10” and “Names some numbers between 1 and 5 when you point to them.” A child who isn’t there yet is not necessarily “behind,” since these are 75 percent milestones. If you have questions about your child’s development in general, their pediatrician is the person to ask.

Pre-K and kindergarten: what schools ask for

Pre-K is the preschool year or two before kindergarten, and kindergarten is the school year before first grade. The Common Core math standards for kindergarten include K.CC.A.1: “Count to 100 by ones and by tens.” That is a goal for the kindergarten year itself, not something kids are expected to arrive with. Whether your state uses Common Core is worth checking with your school or district. If you also speak Spanish at home, counting in both languages is another option: see colors and numbers in Spanish for kids.

Counting games with no materials

At home: the table, the grocery bag, the stairs

DREME suggests counting in everyday situations: handing out toys, playing hide-and-seek, setting the table, putting groceries in the cart, climbing steps, blowing bubbles and looking at books. A few ways to use that:

  • Setting the table: “How many plates do we need? One for you, one for me…” while touching each plate.
  • Groceries: count the pieces of fruit going into the bag together.
  • Stairs and bubbles: count out loud as you climb or pop them, in the same sequence each time.
  • After counting, ask: “How many are there?” (our suggestion) It gives your child a chance to say the total.

Board games with numbered squares

Ramani and Siegler (2008) had preschoolers from low-income families in the US, average age 5.4, play a linear board game with numbered squares. After about 60 minutes of play in total, the kids improved at comparing amounts, placing numbers on a number line, counting and identifying numerals, and the gains were still there nine weeks later. Kids who played with colored squares instead of numbers didn’t improve.

Read it with care: it was one specific game, a few sessions and one kind of family. It doesn’t mean any board game builds math skills. A simple version of our own: draw a path of numbered squares on a sheet of paper, use a button as a game piece, and move one or two squares at a time while counting out loud.

Common mistakes (and how not to stress)

  • Assuming a child can count because they can recite. Ask for three spoons instead of asking them to count to three. Handing over the right number shows whether they grasp the amount.
  • Treating ages as deadlines. Milestones describe what at least 75 percent of kids do, and a quarter of them might not yet.
  • Expecting big amounts to be seen at once. Only a few objects can be recognized at a glance; with more, it helps to see small groups or count. Beyond that, counting is the tool.
  • Turning it into a lesson. The sources we used suggest routines and games, not formal sessions. Counting while doing something else still counts.

Frequently asked questions

At what age do kids count to 10?

The CDC lists “Counts to 10” at age 5, which means 75 percent or more of kids can do it by then. Before that, it is normal for a child to recite part of the sequence without knowing yet how many objects there are. Milestones are a guide drawn from US data, not a deadline or a diagnosis.

My child says the numbers but doesn’t know how many there are. Is that normal?

Yes. According to DREME, knowing that the last number said is the total is a separate idea from reciting the sequence. In the Le Corre and Carey study, kids gave meaning to “one” through “four” before they mastered the principles of counting. It helps to count real objects and then ask, “How many are there?”

What is subitizing?

It is knowing how many objects there are at a glance, without counting each one. DREME says basic subitizing involves very small groups: one, two, three and maybe four. With more, it can help to see smaller groups within them, or to count. You can practice it with everyday things, such as the crackers on a plate or the dots on a die.

Do board games help kids learn numbers?

In one study of preschoolers from low-income US families, a few sessions with a numbered-square board game improved several number skills, while the same game with colored squares did not. The results come from one specific game, so they don’t apply to every board game. A game that puts numbers in order and has kids count as they move is the closest match.

What does kindergarten expect my child to count?

The Common Core kindergarten standard K.CC.A.1 asks kids to “count to 100 by ones and by tens,” and it is a goal for the year, not a requirement at the door. Ask your school or district which standards apply. Our guide to kindergarten readiness covers the rest.
