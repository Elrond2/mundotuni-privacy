Kindergarten Readiness: Early Math and Literacy at Home

Being ready for kindergarten covers much more than the ABCs and counting to 10. Early math and early literacy are the parts families can practice easily at home, through talking, counting and play.

“Ready for kindergarten” has no single definition, and it covers more than letters and numbers. Still, early math and early literacy are the parts of readiness that fit easily into family life, because they grow out of talking, pointing, counting and playing. The CDC’s milestones for age 5 include counting to 10, naming some numbers and letters, and writing some letters of the child’s name. Kindergarten is the school year before first grade, and pre-K is the preschool year or two that come before it.

The short version

  • Checklists online don’t agree on what “ready” means, and most mix social, language, math and motor skills.
  • The CDC’s age 5 milestones describe what 75 percent or more of kids do. They are not an entry exam.
  • Early math and early literacy skills are linked to later learning, but the research shows association, not cause.
  • The Common Core counting goal for kindergarten is for the school year itself, not for the first day.

What does “ready for kindergarten” mean?

If you search for a readiness checklist, you will find lists that disagree. In the ones we looked at, the items run across social and emotional skills, language, math and fine-motor skills, and they don’t weigh letters and numbers equally. We won’t pick a winner among them. What we can do is stay with sources that say what they measure: the CDC, which lists milestones, the Common Core, which sets standards for the kindergarten year, and a few research studies on early skills.

What the CDC lists at age 5

These are the items in the CDC’s age 5 milestones that touch math and literacy. The quotes are the CDC’s wording, and the grouping is ours.

AreaCDC milestone at age 5
Math“Counts to 10”
Math“Names some numbers between 1 and 5 when you point to them”
Literacy“Names some letters when you point to them”
Literacy“Writes some letters in her name”

The milestones were revised with the American Academy of Pediatrics in 2022, and each one reflects what 75 percent or more of kids can do at that age (Zubler et al., 2022). So up to a quarter of 5-year-olds haven’t reached a given item. The milestones are a tool for keeping track of development, not a test. Milestones are not a diagnosis: if your child isn’t meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills, or you have concerns, talk with their pediatrician and ask about developmental screening.

Early math: counting and “how many”

One relevant study is an analysis of six longitudinal studies. Duncan and colleagues (2007) found that math skills at school entry were the strongest predictor of later achievement. That is an association: it does not show that teaching math early causes better results later, and other things travel with early math skills.

For a sense of what schools ask for, the Common Core kindergarten standard K.CC.A.1 reads “Count to 100 by ones and by tens,” and K.OA.A.5 reads “Fluently add and subtract within 5.” Both describe what kids work toward during kindergarten, not what they bring on the first day. Whether your state follows the Common Core is something to check with your school or district.

At home, early math mostly means counting things that matter to your child and asking “how many?” We go into this in our guide to teaching counting to toddlers and preschoolers, including why reciting numbers and counting are different skills.

Early literacy: sounds and letters

Letter names are only part of early literacy. The National Early Literacy Panel (Lonigan and Shanahan, 2008) reviewed research on the early skills that predict later reading and writing. Among the skills it identified were alphabet knowledge, phonological awareness (noticing the sounds inside spoken words), rapid naming, writing one’s own name and phonological memory. The panel reported that these skills kept their predictive value even after controlling for IQ and socioeconomic status.

As with math, that is prediction, not proof that practicing any one skill produces a good reader. It does suggest that early literacy goes beyond reciting the alphabet song. Talking, rhyming, playing with first sounds (“ball starts with b”) and pointing out letters in your child’s name all feed the same skills. Our guide to helping toddler language develop through play has more on the language side.

What kindergarten expects, and what preschool has to teach

Readiness lists tend to blur two questions: what kids should know when they arrive, and what the kindergarten year is there to teach. The standards we cite are written for the year itself. They describe what a teacher will work on, so a child who can’t yet count past 10 in September is not failing a requirement. The CDC milestones, in turn, are written for monitoring, so they don’t set a pass mark either. What your own school does with either one is a question for the school.

Playful practice at home

These ideas are ours, not drawn from a study, and none is a promise of a result:

  • Count what you already handle. Stairs, snack pieces, shoes by the door. Then ask “how many?”
  • Point and name. The CDC’s wording for letters and numbers is “when you point to them,” so signs, cereal boxes and elevator buttons make natural practice.
  • Play with first sounds. “I spy something that starts with mmm.”
  • Let them make marks. Drawing, tracing and writing their name in their own way builds toward writing letters.
  • Read together and talk about the story. Ask what might happen next.

Short and relaxed works better than long and tense. If your child resists, stop and try again another day.

Frequently asked questions

What should my child know before kindergarten?

There is no universal list. The CDC’s age 5 milestones include counting to 10, naming some numbers and letters, and writing some letters of their name, as things that 75 percent or more of kids do. Kindergarten teaches the rest. Your local school can tell you what it expects, and your pediatrician can answer questions about development.

Does my child need to count to 100 before kindergarten?

No. The Common Core standard K.CC.A.1, “Count to 100 by ones and by tens,” is a goal for the kindergarten year itself. Separately, the CDC lists counting to 10 as a milestone for age 5. Check with your school or district which standards your state follows.

Are early math skills more important than reading skills?

The research doesn’t rank them for any individual child. Duncan and colleagues found that math skills at school entry were the strongest predictor of later achievement in six longitudinal studies, and the National Early Literacy Panel found that several early literacy skills predict later reading. Both findings are associations, and both areas are worth some playful time.

My child doesn’t know their letters yet. Should I worry?

Not on that fact alone. The CDC milestone, “Names some letters when you point to them,” is for age 5 and reflects what 75 percent or more of kids do, so some 5-year-olds haven’t got there yet. Keep practicing in short, relaxed moments. Milestones are not a diagnosis: if your child isn’t meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills, or you have concerns, talk with their pediatrician and ask about developmental screening.

What is the difference between pre-K and kindergarten?

Pre-K is the preschool year or two before kindergarten, and kindergarten is the school year before first grade. Ask your school or district how they define them and when your child is eligible.
