The Spanish Alphabet for Kids: Letters, Names and Sounds

The Spanish alphabet has 27 letters, and most of them will look familiar. This guide gives each letter’s name, an example word and the sounds that differ from English, plus simple games for a toddler or preschooler.

The Spanish alphabet has 27 letters: the 26 you know plus ñ. According to the Real Academia Española (RAE), the letters are a, b, c, d, e, f, g, h, i, j, k, l, m, n, ñ, o, p, q, r, s, t, u, v, w, x, y, z. Spanish spelling also uses combinations such as ch and ll, but the RAE no longer counts them as letters of the alphabet. This guide gives each letter’s name, an example word and the sounds that differ from English, plus games to play with a toddler or preschooler.

The short version

  • 27 letters, with the ñ between n and o.
  • Ch and ll were counted as letters from 1803 until the RAE’s 2010 spelling book; today they are digraphs, pairs of letters that stand for one sound.
  • B and v sound the same in Spanish, and h is silent.
  • Some letter names and a few sounds change by region, so use this guide as a map and not as a rule.
  • Keep it playful and short; the games below need no small objects, and an adult stays next to the child.

How many letters are in the Spanish alphabet?

Twenty-seven. The RAE says so in its explanation of why ch and ll left the alphabet: the 2010 Ortografía de la lengua española excluded them because they are not letters but digraphs, sets of two letters that represent a single sound. The Academy’s Diccionario panhispánico de dudas (DPD) adds that they were traditionally counted as letters, named che and elle. The ch entry says that the sign was considered a letter from 1803 until the 2010 spelling book.

This does not mean ch and ll disappeared from writing. They are still used in words such as chico (boy) and calle (street). An older alphabet that counted ch and ll as letters had 29, which is why the question still comes up.

The letters, their names and example words

The names are from the DPD, one entry per letter. The “rough sound” column is our own approximation for English speakers, not an official respelling. For anything you’re unsure about, listen to an online dictionary with audio. The example words are our own picks; a few are chosen because the DPD uses them to illustrate the sound.

Two more pairs you will meet in words: ch (che in the old alphabet) as in chico, which the RAE says stands for one sound, and ll (elle) as in calle. Some speakers say ll as a separate sound and others say it like y, and both are heard. The DPD calls the digraph rr erre doble or doble erre.

The five vowels: a, e, i, o, u

The vowel names are the letters themselves: a, e, i, o, u. Start here: five letters, each with a rough English sound in the table above. You can hear them in words like mamá, oso and uva. Say each vowel slowly, hold it for a moment, and let your child copy the mouth shape. Don’t worry about getting it perfect.

Sounds that differ from English

  • H is silent. The DPD says h represents no sound in standard Spanish. Helado starts with the sound of e.
  • B and v sound the same. The DPD says there is no difference in pronunciation between the two letters; pairs such as baca and vaca sound exactly alike.
  • J, and g before e or i, make the same raspy sound, a bit like a strong English h. The DPD notes that in southern Spain, the Canary Islands and large areas of Latin America it tends to be softer.
  • Ñ is like the “ny” in “canyon,” but in one smooth sound.
  • Z, and c before e or i. In central, northern and eastern Spain they sound like the “th” in “thin”; in the southwest of Spain, the Canary Islands and all of Latin America, like “s.” The DPD calls this seseo.
  • R and rr. According to the DPD, r between vowels (cara) is a quick tap, while r at the start of a word (reto) and rr between vowels (carro) are rolled.
  • Q comes with a silent u: queso is KEH-so, and qu makes the “k” sound before e and i.

Names that change by region

For some letters the DPD recommends one name and lists the regional ones. Here are the examples we checked in its entries:

  • V: uve is recommended because it can be told apart from be without extra words. In the Americas you may also hear ve, ve corta, ve chica, ve pequeña and ve baja.
  • B: be, and in the Americas also be larga, be grande and be alta.
  • W: uve doble is preferred, and doble uve is less common. In the Americas you may also hear ve doble and doble ve, and in Mexico and some Central American and Caribbean countries, doble u, copied from English.
  • Y: the DPD lists both ye and i griega, so a child may hear either one.

These differences are about letter names, not about how words sound. Pick whichever you hear around you and use it for a while. We discuss the same question for words in Spain and Latin American Spanish.

How to practice with toddlers and preschoolers

These are our own ideas, none tested as a program. Stay next to your child for all of them, keep sessions short, and stop when the fun stops. Skip anything small enough to fit in a child’s mouth: no beads, buttons, bottle caps or tiny magnetic letters. Everything below uses only your voice, your body, a big crayon or chalk and big pictures. For first sounds in general, see our guide on early reading and math at home.

  1. Veo, veo (I spy) with first sounds. “Veo, veo, algo que empieza con /m/” (I spy something that starts with m). Point to mesa (table) or mono. Say the sound, and the letter name too if you like.
  2. Name hunt. Find the first letter of your child’s name on a cereal box, a sign or a book cover, and say its Spanish name together: eme, ese. Keep to one letter per day.
  3. Big letters in the air. Draw an a or an o in the air with a big arm, then on paper with a thick crayon, or on the sidewalk with chalk. Say the vowel as you draw.
  4. Letter with your body. Make an o with your arms, an i standing tall, an a with your legs apart like a tent. Take turns, and let your child be the one who guesses.
  5. The silent h game. Say helado, hola and hoja, and ask your child to put a finger on their lips for the quiet h. A silent letter makes a fun game.
  6. Picture and echo. With a large picture book or a poster, point to a picture and say the word, then the first letter: gato, ge. Your child echoes any part they like. Let them point and name things they know in English too.

If you don’t speak Spanish, you don’t need to be perfect: say the words you know and use an online dictionary with audio for the rest. Our guide for parents who don’t speak Spanish has more.

Frequently asked questions

How many letters are in the Spanish alphabet?

The RAE lists 27: the 26 letters of the English alphabet plus ñ, which comes after n.

Are ch and ll still letters in Spanish?

Not in the alphabet. The RAE says its 2010 spelling book excluded them as letters because they are digraphs, two letters that stand for one sound. They are still used in words like chico and calle, and words that start with them are listed under c and l. Older books may still show a 29-letter alphabet.

What is the letter y called in Spanish?

The DPD says ye or i griega. Both are in use. The DPD says the i is also called i latina to tell it apart when the y is called i griega.

Are b and v pronounced differently?

No. According to the DPD, they represent the same sound in Spanish, so baca and vaca sound the same. The DPD recommends the name uve for the v because it can be told apart from be without extra words.

Do I need to speak Spanish to try these games?

No. Start with the five vowels and a handful of words from the table, say them often, and check anything you’re unsure about in an online dictionary with audio. Our first Spanish words for toddlers and colors and numbers in Spanish give more words to play with.

Which accent should I use for the letters?

Whichever you hear or feel comfortable with. The differences are small (the names of v, w and y, and z or c as “th” or “s”), and the DPD lists them as regional variants. See Spain and Latin American Spanish if you want to choose.
