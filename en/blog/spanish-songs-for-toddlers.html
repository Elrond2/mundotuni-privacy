Spanish Songs for Toddlers: What They Do and How to Use Them

Songs are one of the easiest ways to add Spanish to a toddler’s week, even if you don’t speak it. Here is what research on songs can and can’t tell you, seven traditional songs to start with and how to use them without a screen.

If you want your toddler to hear Spanish and you aren’t sure where to start, songs are a gentle way in. They are short, they repeat, and many come with motions. They are also something you can do together, which matters more than it sounds. Songs won’t teach your child Spanish by themselves, and we found no study that says they will. Below is what we do know, and a few songs to try tonight.

The short version

  • In one small study, children learned to recognize words from a song, but most could not yet say them.
  • Studies on language learning favor people who respond to the child over recordings that play on their own.
  • A large review found that music training doesn’t raise children’s general cognitive or school skills. Sing for the fun of it.
  • Sing, move and pause so your child can answer. If you use videos, keep screen time low, as WHO and AAP guidance asks.

Do songs help toddlers learn Spanish?

We found no study that tested Spanish songs with English-speaking toddlers at home. The closest we found goes in the opposite direction. Coyle and Gómez Gracia (2014) taught English words to 25 five-year-olds in Spain through three 30-minute lessons built around a well-known children’s song. Picture tests given before and after the lessons, and again five weeks later, suggested that the children’s receptive vocabulary improved: they could recognize the words. Most children did not show productive knowledge, meaning they could not yet use the words themselves.

The limits matter. The group was small, the children were older than most toddlers, and from the summary we read we can’t say how a song compares with other ways of teaching the same words. Our reading is modest: a song can be a pleasant way to hear a few words over and over, and speaking them takes more, such as conversation with someone who answers. Notice, too, that the study used one song across three lessons. Repeating a favorite is not a problem.

Why singing along beats pressing play

Other studies, not about songs, point the same way. Kuhl and colleagues (2003) found that 9-month-old American babies learned Mandarin speech sounds from live speakers but not from audio or video recordings. Roseberry and colleagues (2014) tested 36 children aged 24 to 30 months on new verbs, and the children learned from interactions that responded to them, in person or on video chat, but not from video that didn’t respond. We cover both in how to teach your toddler Spanish if you don’t speak it, with their limits.

A study of infants adds a related clue. Gerry and colleagues (2012) randomly assigned babies, starting at 6 months, to six months of active, participatory music classes or to passive exposure to music. The active group showed better development of early communicative gestures and social behavior. Those were babies, not toddlers, and the study wasn’t about learning Spanish. We don’t see it as proof that singing teaches a language. It is one more reason to sing with your child instead of leaving a recording on.

Our inference, which no study tested: a parent who sings along, points and pauses for the child to fill in a word is being responsive, and that fits what these studies favor. Hoff and colleagues (2012) also found that, in bilingual toddlers, skills in each language went with how much of that language the child heard. Songs are an easy way to add a little Spanish most days.

Don’t expect songs to make your child smarter

A meta-analysis of 54 studies with children (Sala and Gobet, 2020) concluded that, once the quality of study design is taken into account, the overall effect of music training on cognitive skills and academic achievement was null. That is a finding about music training, not about singing nursery rhymes at bath time, but it is a good reason to keep expectations modest. Sing Spanish songs because they bring Spanish into your home and are fun to do together.

Seven traditional Spanish songs to start with

These are traditional songs sung in many Spanish-speaking countries. We haven’t traced where each one comes from, and words vary by country and family, so don’t worry if a recording you find differs from what we quote. Listen to the song a few times before singing it with your child, so you can borrow the pronunciation.

  • Los pollitos dicen. It begins “Los pollitos dicen: pío, pío, pío.” Flap your arms like wings and make the chick sounds. Words you pick up: hambre (hungry), frío (cold), comida (food).
  • La araña pequeñita. It begins “La araña pequeñita subió, subió, subió.” Walk your fingers up your child’s arm, then wiggle them down like rain. If you know “Itsy Bitsy Spider,” the story will feel familiar. Words: subir (to climb), lluvia (rain), sol (sun).
  • Cabeza, hombros, rodillas y pies. Touch each body part as you sing. The CDC’s 2-year milestones include pointing to at least two body parts when asked, and its tips for that age suggest teaching body parts through a song. Words: cabeza, hombros, rodillas, pies.
  • Debajo de un botón. It begins “Debajo de un botón, ton, ton, que encontró Martín, tin, tin.” The rhyming sound play makes it fun to say. Words: botón (button), ratón (mouse).
  • Cucú, cantaba la rana. It begins “Cucú, cantaba la rana, cucú, debajo del agua.” Hop like a frog and sing quietly, then loudly. Words: rana (frog), agua (water).
  • Un elefante se balanceaba. Swing your arm like a trunk. Each verse adds one more elephant, so it counts up in Spanish: un elefante, dos elefantes. Words: uno, dos, tres.
  • Si tú tienes muchas ganas de aplaudir. If you know “If You’re Happy and You Know It,” you can follow along: clap, stomp, shout. Words: aplaudir (to clap), pie (foot).

Not sure how a word sounds? Look for recordings by native Spanish speakers. Voices from Spain and Latin America differ a little, but the songs are the same. We explain the differences in Spain vs. Latin American Spanish for kids, and the short version is that you can pick the voices you can find and enjoy.

How to use songs without a screen

These ideas are ours. None of the studies above tested them.

  • Pick one song and one moment. The bath, the car, the walk to the park. A routine is easier to keep than a plan to “play more Spanish.”
  • Use audio, not video, when you can. A speaker and your own voice are enough, and the song doesn’t need a picture.
  • Sing it together, with motions. Your child can copy the motion long before they can say the word.
  • Pause before the last word. Sing “Los pollitos dicen: pío, pío…” and wait. If your child answers in English, accept it and keep singing in Spanish.
  • Repeat the favorite. Many toddlers ask for the same song again and again. That is fine, and it is what the song study did too, with three lessons around a single song.

If you do play videos, the WHO advises no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day for ages 2 to 4, and says less is better. The AAP’s 2026 guidance looks beyond time to the content, how the child uses it and how the app or platform is designed. We go through both in our screen-time article.

Frequently asked questions

Can my toddler learn Spanish just from songs?

Not by songs alone, as far as we can tell. In the study we cite, songs helped children recognize words, but most could not use them yet. The research on language learning that we cite points to hearing a lot of the language and to live, back-and-forth interaction.

Do I need to sing in tune or speak Spanish?

None of the studies we cite tested singing ability or the parent’s Spanish. They point to doing it together and responding to your child. Listen to a recording by a native speaker first, and sing along with it at first if you prefer.

My toddler wants the same song over and over. Is that okay?

Yes. The song study had children revisit one song across three lessons. No source we use says how many repetitions are right, so follow your child’s interest.

Do Spanish songs make toddlers smarter?

No evidence supports that. A meta-analysis of 54 studies found that, once study quality was accounted for, music training had no overall effect on children’s cognitive skills or academic achievement. Songs are worth singing for the Spanish, the closeness and the fun.

Is it okay to use YouTube videos of Spanish songs?

That is your family’s decision. The WHO advises no more than one hour of sedentary screen time a day for ages 2 to 4, and less is better. If you do watch, watch together and choose calm content. An audio-only version of the same song needs no screen at all.

Which Spanish should I choose?

Spanish from Spain and Latin America are the same language with different accents and some different words. The songs above are sung in many countries, so any native-speaker recording works. See Spain vs. Latin American Spanish for kids.
