Bilingual Speech Delay: Do Two Languages Cause It?

No. Learning two languages does not cause a speech or language delay. It is common, though, for each language to grow a little more slowly on its own, and for your child to mix the two for a while.

No. Raising a child with two languages does not cause a speech or language delay. The Communication Health Support Association (CHSA), with the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA), says that using more than one language does not confuse children or cause delays, and the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says the same. What can happen is that your toddler is a bit behind a monolingual peer in each language taken separately, and that they blend the two for a while.

The short version

  • The first communication milestones, such as laughing, smiling and babbling, are the same in every language.
  • When both languages are counted together, vocabulary is comparable to a monolingual child’s. Counted one language at a time, each may look smaller.
  • Mixing languages in a sentence is expected and does not mean your child is confused.
  • If you are worried about how your child is communicating, ask for an evaluation. Bilingualism is not the explanation.

Does learning two languages delay when kids start talking?

The two US sources we checked agree on the core point. Both are public-education pages for families, not clinical guidelines.

What CHSA and ASHA say

The CHSA page, backed by ASHA and reviewed in May 2026, states that “using multiple languages does not confuse children or cause speech or language delays.” It adds that mixing words or rules from different languages is “an expected part” of learning more than one. It also advises families to speak to their child in the language or languages they feel most comfortable with.

What the AAP says

The AAP page, written with ASHA and updated in April 2024, says early communication milestones are the same across languages. If a child uses more than one language, the total number of words should be about the same as for a child who uses one. The AAP also advises adults to speak in the language they are most comfortable with.

Where the sources stop

Neither page sets its own milestone ages for bilingual children. The milestones you will see quoted, such as the CDC’s, are general ones. Both pages are written for parents, so treat them as guidance, not a diagnostic tool.

Do bilingual kids know fewer words?

It depends on how you count. Hoff and colleagues (2012) studied toddlers aged 22 to 30 months from higher-income families in the United States. The monolingual children were ahead in vocabulary and grammar in a single language, while the two groups were even in total vocabulary. In the bilingual children, the level in each language went with how much of that language they heard. More relative exposure meant more progress in it.

Place and Hoff (2011) followed a similar pattern with 29 bilingual 25-month-olds. Relative exposure to each language predicted how well they did in it. In English, the number of different speakers and the share of input from native speakers also counted. The Oxford Brookes BabyLab reaches the same conclusion: the gap in vocabulary disappears when both languages are added up.

So when you check how your child is doing, look at everything they say and understand across both languages. Comparing each language with what a monolingual child would do gives a misleading picture.

Is it normal for a toddler to mix two languages in one sentence?

Yes. CHSA calls it an expected part of learning more than one language, and the AAP says it does not mean the child is confused. On timing, the AAP says that by about age 3, multilingual children can generally keep their languages apart. We found no other US source that gives a firmer age, so read it as a rough range, not a deadline.

If you are wondering about a child who understands one language and answers in another, we cover that in when your child understands Spanish but answers in English.

What if the parents are the ones mixing?

Here the science has not settled the question, and it would not be honest to say that parental mixing is harmless or that it hurts. Three studies, three results:

  • In 181 families, more mixing by parents went with a significantly smaller receptive vocabulary at 18 months (Byers-Heinlein, 2013). The study is correlational and relies on what parents report.
  • In 398 children aged 7 to 34 months in Montreal, the associations were minimal (Paquette and Byers-Heinlein, 2025).
  • In 52 children aged 3 to 5, there was no link with the majority language, but a negative link with expressive vocabulary in the minority language (Verhoeven and colleagues, 2026). The sample is small and the design cross-sectional.

All three measure associations, not causes. For most families, the practical takeaway from CHSA and the AAP stands: talk to your child in the language you are most comfortable with.

Do you have to follow “one parent, one language”?

No. The Oxford Brookes BabyLab describes that approach as neither necessary nor sufficient for successful bilingual acquisition. What seems to matter is how much your child hears of each language, as the exposure studies above suggest. If you are trying to keep a home language strong, our article on raising bilingual kids and keeping Spanish goes into the everyday side of it.

When should you ask for an evaluation?

If your child is not reaching speech milestones for their age, do not wait to see whether bilingualism explains it. CHSA notes that a real communication problem shows up in all the languages a child uses, so a child who struggles in both is worth checking. When in doubt, it recommends talking to a speech-language pathologist.

The CDC lists, among the milestones at 2 years, that a child says at least two words together, like “More milk.” If any milestone is missing, its advice is “Don’t wait.” Those milestones come from the United States and do not separate out bilingual children, so they are a starting point for a conversation with your pediatrician. They are not a test. Your pediatrician can refer you to early intervention or to a speech-language pathologist if needed.

Does being bilingual make kids smarter?

That has not been shown. A meta-analysis of studies with children (Gunnerud and colleagues, 2020) found a significant but marginal effect on executive functions, with signs of publication bias. In adults, another meta-analysis (Lehtonen and colleagues, 2018) concluded that, after correcting for that bias, no evidence of an advantage remained. Bilingualism has value for communication, family and identity. We can’t guarantee a boost in test scores.

Frequently asked questions

Do bilingual kids start talking later?

Not necessarily. According to the AAP, the first communication milestones, like laughing, smiling and babbling, are the same in every language. CHSA says using more than one language does not cause speech or language delays. If your child is missing milestones for their age, mention it to your pediatrician instead of waiting.

Is it normal for my toddler to mix English and Spanish?

Yes. CHSA describes mixing words or rules from different languages as an expected part of learning more than one, and the AAP says it does not mean your child is confused. The AAP adds that, by about age 3, multilingual children can generally keep their languages apart. Treat that as a rough guide.

Will my bilingual child have a smaller vocabulary?

In each language on its own, possibly. With both languages counted together, vocabulary is comparable to a monolingual child’s. Hoff and colleagues (2012) found this in toddlers aged 22 to 30 months, and the Oxford Brookes BabyLab reaches the same conclusion. That is why it helps to look at everything your child says across both languages.

Do I have to use “one parent, one language”?

No. The Oxford Brookes BabyLab calls it neither necessary nor sufficient for bilingual success. CHSA and the AAP both advise adults to speak in the language or languages they are most comfortable with. What counts is that your child hears each language often and in real conversation.

When should I talk to a professional?

As soon as you see that your child is not reaching milestones for their age. Don’t wait. The CDC advises not to wait if one is missing, and CHSA says a real problem appears in all of a child’s languages. Start with your pediatrician, who can refer you to a speech-language pathologist or to early intervention.
